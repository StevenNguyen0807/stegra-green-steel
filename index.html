Green steel · Relational view

Green steel can't be built alone: what Stegra's near-collapse teaches us about partnerships

Sweden's flagship green steel plant has survived two funding crises in a year. Its story shows that net zero in heavy industry depends less on single firms, and more on the relationships between them.

Nguyễn Xuân Duy Thịnh, International Business student, Swinburne University of Technology VietnamNovember 2026

In Boden, a small town near the Arctic Circle in northern Sweden, Europe's boldest climate bet is rising out of the forest. Stegra, a start-up founded in 2020, is building the world's first large-scale steel plant that runs on green hydrogen instead of coal. It has also come close to running out of money twice in twelve months.

In late 2025, the company was searching for up to €975 million to cover rising costs. In April 2026, a consortium led by Sweden's Wallenberg family agreed a €1.4 billion rescue. Then in September 2026, Stegra said it needed even more capital and replaced its chief executive.

This looks like one more clean-tech start-up in trouble. A closer look tells a different story: Stegra exists because of its partnerships, and it has survived because of them too.

COST

Why steel matters for the climate

Construction is responsible for about a quarter of global emissions and uses more than half of the world's steel, according to McKinsey. About 73% of steel is still made in coal-fired blast furnaces.

Making steel with hydrogen, powered by renewable electricity, can cut those emissions to almost zero. The catch is cost. To pay for a new hydrogen-based plant, green steel would need a "green premium" of 20 to 25% over 20 years. McKinsey's model of an eight-storey office building finds that cutting its embodied carbon by about 70%, with greener steel alongside other low-carbon materials, would raise material costs by only around 5%.

That gap is the heart of the problem. No firm wants to carry the premium alone, even though the cost to the final product is small. This makes green steel a direct test of the UN's Sustainable Development Goal 9 (industry, innovation and infrastructure) and Goal 13 (climate action).

TRUST

A plant built on relationships

In a classic 1998 paper, strategy scholars Jeffrey Dyer and Harbir Singh argued that firms can earn advantages together that none could achieve alone. They called these "relational rents" and traced them to four sources: investments made specifically for a partner, knowledge sharing, complementary resources, and effective governance that builds trust.

Stegra fits this "relational view" almost perfectly. By late 2024, before producing a single tonne, it had signed 20 binding offtake contracts, long-term agreements to buy its steel, with customers such as Mercedes-Benz, Porsche, Scania and IKEA. Together these covered about 60% of the plant's first-phase output, for up to seven years. Banks then accepted the contracts as security for the debt that funds around two-thirds of the €6.5 billion project. The relationships were, quite literally, the collateral.

A striking construction example: in 2025 Microsoft agreed to work with its suppliers so that Stegra steel goes into some of its European data centres. Research on green steel markets finds that construction has been slow to adopt it because its supply chains are fragmented, and subcontractors cut costs unless the client at the top demands greener materials. Microsoft is that client. Its purchasing power complements Stegra's technology: a textbook case of complementary resources.

The relational view, revisited

The relational view has a darker side. Partners who invest specifically in each other become dependent on each other. A later update of the theory warns that the things which create value early on, such as trust and customised investments, can later weigh on performance, while partners compete over how the gains are shared.

At Stegra, the web of loans, grants and supply contracts began to strain. Costs rose, and a Swedish government agency withdrew €165 million of previously approved grant funding, reportedly over the plant's planned use of some fossil gas. By October 2025, sources were telling the Financial Times that the crisis was starting to resemble Northvolt, the Swedish battery maker whose bankruptcy had already shaken the country's green industry. When every partner's money depends on the others, one weak link threatens the whole chain.

What held the network together? Largely the same relationships that created the risk. Existing shareholders backed the 2026 rescue, joined by new investors including Singapore's Temasek. Management researcher Stephan Duschek argues that the resources behind such advantages often span firm boundaries, so no single partner can generate them alone. That helps explain why investors rescued the network rather than walking away.

Governance changed too. A former Volvo chief executive was brought in as chair, and in September 2026 a Wallenberg executive took over as CEO. The new chair said the company had found more partnership opportunities than expected. In relational terms, the network rewired its governance to protect the value it had built.

Why Vietnam should be watching

For Vietnamese steelmakers, this is not a distant European story. On 1 January 2026, the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism entered its definitive regime, so importers must now buy certificates for the carbon embedded in the steel they bring in. Vietnam shipped more than 2.5 million tonnes of steel to the EU in 2023, 23% of its steel exports. The WTO estimated the carbon border tax could cut that export value by about 4%.

Stegra's lesson is that cleaning up steel is not just a technology purchase. It needs committed buyers, patient investors and clear rules on who pays the premium. That is what SDG 17, partnerships for the goals, calls for, and it supports responsible production under SDG 12. Producers such as Hòa Phát, whose hot-rolled coil drove the country's export growth in 2023, should take note. Those that build such partnerships early, with foreign buyers who need low-carbon steel, could turn a carbon tax into a market opportunity.

The bottom line

Stegra may still stumble. Its start date is under review and its bill keeps rising. But whatever happens in Boden, its story makes one point clear: net zero in heavy industry will not be delivered by heroic companies acting alone. It will be delivered by networks, and those networks need careful governance to survive bad news.
